When the film comes, I'll probably be thinking about The Magician's Nephew — the original point and story. What does that say about me? I'm for sure going to be re-reading it, and discovering it anew, in all its beauty and power to guide us to Jesus, who is God.

Meryl Streep is going to voice Aslan. That is confirmed now, for Greta Gerwig's Narnia: The Magician's Nephew — in cinemas 12 February 2027, then on Netflix 2 April 2027.

People are arguing about it. Some say Aslan should sound like a man, like a king. Some say a woman's voice is wrong for him. Some say the voice does not matter, because Aslan is not a man.

Lewis answered this himself, in a letter in December 1958, to a Mrs Hook. He wrote:

"If Aslan represented the immaterial Deity in the same way in which Giant Despair represents Despair, he would be an allegorical figure. In reality however he is an invention giving an imaginary answer to the question, 'What might Christ become like, if there really were a world like Narnia and He chose to be incarnate and die and rise again in that world as He actually has done in ours?' This is not allegory at all."

He called the books supposals — a "what if":

"Let us suppose that there were a land like Narnia and that the Son of God, as he became a Man in our world, became a Lion there, and then imagine what would happen."

Now, here is the question I keep coming back to. Lewis had strong ideas even about the drawings of his characters. He worked closely with Pauline Baynes, who drew Narnia — and privately he complained that she could not draw lions, and that she knew nothing about animal anatomy. To her face he asked for only one small change in all those years, a man rowing a boat the wrong way round. And when her work won the Carnegie Medal, he wrote back, "Is it not rather 'our' Medal?"

So we might guess what he would think of a woman voicing Aslan. Or we might not. He cared deeply about how Aslan looked and sounded in his head — and he still let someone else draw him, and shared the credit. You never really know what a writer would say about a film made seventy years later. I think it is honest to say that, and to leave the question open.

I understand why people disagree, and for the most part it is probably healthy to have this theological debate — about a movie — that is about a great book, which leads us to an excellent shifting of our mind toward the good news. It is how Lewis helps us, much like Tolkien, to reimagine the scriptural story of who Jesus is — and not stay on the sidelines, but step into life in Jesus.

If you wonder where I stand on all of this — the film, the casting, the book — watch what I said in the video. That is where I stand.

Here it is, in my own words. From the video:

"My thought is that Aslan is so good it's impossible to portray him in a way that completely explains what Lewis was going for. Lewis himself did the best he could to explain who God is."

And from the longer piece I wrote:

"I reckon Jesus is bigger than all of this, and He is reaching out to people. I want the conversation to lean back into Jesus."

I made that video because this is the point for me: a whole new group of people are going to meet Digory and Polly and Aslan for the first time. And I am just happy we are talking about faith in Jesus because of these books by Lewis. If the film sends people back to the book, that is the best thing it could do. Some people have not read The Magician's Nephew yet. If that is you — stop what you are doing and read it first, then go and watch the movie by all means.

I also completely see the point that people may never read the books after watching the movie, and the movie may cause a whole lot of people to miss the entire point. That would be tragic. It is hard to know yet. Once again, see what I think in the video — it is kind of hard to summarise it here, and you can hear my tone in the video better.

I wrote a longer piece on the book itself — the song, the bell, the apple. Here is a short part of it, the moment Narnia begins:

"In the darkness something was happening at last. A voice had begun to sing."

Then the Lion speaks over the new world:

"Narnia, Narnia, Narnia, awake. Love. Think. Speak. Be walking trees. Be talking beasts. Be divine waters."

So I am asking you. What do you think — does the voice matter? Is Aslan an allegory to you, or something else? And what stayed with you from The Magician's Nephew?
