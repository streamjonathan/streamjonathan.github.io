Church today was extraordinary. Time flowed, meandered, listening to friends reading Jesus, word after word; children exploring the wide open vastness of our spring-in-winter sanctuary. We waved in greeting from our grassy pews to St. James Way pilgrims, picnicking families, and the local shepherd.

Jesus’ words rang. The lost sheep, the lost son. The shepherd herded his flock along the lush Basque hills—stories alive before our very eyes. I saw myself, a sheep; I saw myself in that recovered son. And I saw the one son angered at the other’s return and salvation, angered at the lavish celebration.

When we packed our few things to come make this land our home, seed-sowing friends warned that Basques are “closed and cold”, the land is “dark”. Instead, we have found only kindness, and a welcoming warmth. This shepherd land, like all lands, pastures curiosity for Jesus, receptivity to relationship rather than religion.

At a Christian gathering in the heart of the Basque land, I looked up, around, listened to Spanish sounds, timidly whispered, “Jesus, surely you speak Basque, too?” My unspoken question received a stinging reply from my Spanish kin: “Their language is associated with freedom seeking, radical Basque culture, so to preach, or teach, or even reach in the Basque language would compromise the gospel.” My heart mourned when another said: “Those extremist prisoners released, though they did their time, should be locked back up.” Such remarks become the hurt and the compromise they seek to prevent.

Our friends here read Jesus’ stories in Basque. Our town’s streets ring with this ancient language—children giggling at the plaza, families meeting in the bar, bank clerks and fishermen working at their jobs, the priest saying Mass. Jesus is there, in the fields with his sheep, calling us to join them in the pastures and nuzzle them toward the Shepherd of all the nations.

Amazed, we watched the sheep story unfold. The shepherd with his dog led the sheep to better pasture, further on and further in. One roamed to the side, stopped to nibble, soon was left behind, alone. Not one minute later our children cheered to see the shepherd return, guide that one back to the path. That’s me. That’s the Basque Country.
