I watched a video this week that stayed with me. It is by Marcus Olang', a writer in Nairobi, for New York Times Opinion. It is about what it means to write with a "human touch" — and why that question is harder than it sounds.

It starts with a client. Marcus had worked for days on a project proposal. Proper days. The client sent it back and asked for a rewrite with a more human touch. It sounded, they said, as if it was written by ChatGPT.

His answer is the heart of the video: "I don't write like ChatGPT. ChatGPT writes like me."

<img src="/images/gif-human-touch-1.gif" alt="Marcus Olang' on a dark stage: Last year, I sent off a project proposal that I'd worked on for days" loading="lazy">

He learned his English in Mrs Amolo's classroom in Nairobi, when he was about nine. The Queen's English, he calls it — a colonial relic. The rules were clear. Show off your vocabulary. You never just walked, you strolled. You never just saw a thing, you beheld a magnificent spectacle. A simile in every paragraph. Make your point in threes. And the long sentence — the longer, the better. Every thought telegraphed, announced and delivered, until it arrives, finally, gracefully, completely, at its full stop.

<img src="/images/gif-human-touch-2.gif" alt="Marcus Olang' speaking to camera: Mrs. Amolo spoke in a careful, considered accent" loading="lazy">

All of it built to one moment: the national exam at the end of primary school. Forty minutes to write a single original story by hand. Your chance at a good high school, then a good university, then a good career. Which is to say, the rest of your life.

Then came ChatGPT. It was trained on centuries of formal published English — the books, the journals, Shakespeare, the scripture. The same register Mrs Amolo drilled into her students. So the machine came out the other side sounding like the best student she ever had.

And then the detectors came. The software that is supposed to catch AI writing. Researchers tested it on essays by non-native English speakers, and it flagged more than half of them as AI-written. Almost none of the essays by American students got flagged.

The word "delve" is his example. To a detector, it is a robot word. In Lagos and Nairobi, it is just English — ordinary, everyday, taught for generations. It comes from Old English, to dig. Shakespeare used it in Hamlet. And when the AI companies needed cheap labour to teach their machines good writing from bad, guess who they hired? Data workers in Kenya, for a couple of dollars an hour — people who picked the answer that sounded like the good writing they were taught. The one with words like "delve", a couple of hundred times a day.

So the loop goes round. A classroom teaches a way of writing. A machine learns it, partly taught by us. A second machine is built to catch the first one, and it flags the very things that were drilled into us. Then we are sold a third machine, a humaniser, to rough our English back up — chop a long sentence here, drop a "furthermore" there — so the second machine will finally accept that we are human.

<img src="/images/gif-human-touch-3.gif" alt="Marcus Olang' speaking to camera, hands together: So how did this everyday word in Nairobi become a chatbot's biggest tell?" loading="lazy">

His ending is the part I keep thinking about. Human was never a real place to reach. It is just the word for whatever we happen to be. The perfect is no longer human. The error is. And the whole panic over catching AI was never just about technology. It is about how we define what human is in the first place.

That is why I kept this video. This is how I want to sound — like me, not like a machine. Short, plain, real. If a sentence could have come from anyone, it is not mine.
