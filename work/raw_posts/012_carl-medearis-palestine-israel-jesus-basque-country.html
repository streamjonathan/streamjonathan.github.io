Carl Medearis on Palestine, Israel and Jesus (Basque Country)

October 24, 2013 by Jonathan McCallum

carl medearis


Good evening. My name’s Carl Medearis and this is my wife, Christine. We arrived here one hour ago, so we’re experts on the Basque Country now, as we arrived here an hour ago. (laughter) Somebody asked us what we knew about Basques and I said, “We read one book, we’ve done lots of internet research, and we asked lots of questions of our friends Jonathan and Sarah.” So, mostly, honestly, we’ve come here to learn and eat your food. (laughter) I’m actually an international expert on food eating. As you can see. (laughter)

Something has interested me for years about the Basque people because when I grew up, there were two stories in the Medearis family. One story said that we were Greek and the other story said that we were Basque. And actually until now we don’t know, we can’t trace our history back, so we actually don’t know what it is. So, I’m happy to be Basque. Is Medearis, does that sound Basque to anyone here? No? No, well I must be Greek! (laughter) I came all the way here to answer that question. That’s good.

My wife and I and our family lived in the Middle East for many years. We actually lived in Beirut, Lebanon for twelve years. And we’ve been going in and out of the Middle East for 30 years. I first went there in 1983 to a country called Yemen.

I hope you don’t mind tonight, but my strategy with you is to be very honest with you about our story. I hope you don’t mind. Many years ago we went to the Middle East from a conservative Protestant Christian background to be missionaries to Muslims in the Middle East. I don’t know, somehow I missed the idea that Muslims and Christians of the Middle East, Orthodox and Catholic already had their own religion and they really weren’t very interested in adopting a new American religion. I somehow missed that in my readings and my studies somehow. I think some of that was American arrogance—part of why I missed that point was because Americans are arrogant and I was arrogant. And some of it was just the culture I grew up in, saying that we were right. My religion is right, my nationality is right, and other people are wrong, so I need to tell them that they’re wrong and invite them to be right like me. (laughter) Yeah, it’s ridiculous. And I don’t know why I thought that was such a good idea, but at the time it seemed like a really good idea.

And soon after, I think maybe after year 2 or 3 of trying really hard to make everyone in the world protestant Americans; I started to realize the issues of power mixed with religion and how dangerous that is. Political power mixed with religious power being given to people who have no power—how dangerous that is. I kept thinking, “You know, my heart is good, so surely they’ll understand that even though I’m a big loud American, like all the stereotypes, surely they’ll understand that I mean well.” But they never did understand that. They just saw a big loud American.

I started to see that the power structure in the Middle East is: Israel has all the power backed by America, so Christian America with Jewish Israel has all the power. And then I kinda walked into the middle of that, announcing what I thought was the good news of American Christianity, but not realizing that that’s not actually good news at all to anyone, it’s actually bad news. For the first time I realized that there are people called Palestinians who don’t live in Palestine anymore, who have been made refugees and now live in Lebanon, Syria and Jordan but are Palestinian. Somehow in all my training I also missed that point—that there are millions of Palestinians who no longer live in Palestine and that my country has something to do with that. I started to realize that when the Palestinians would react to having their land and their homes taken they would be called terrorists by the people who had the power. And it was the first time I realized that that’s not only not the whole story but that’s actually the wrong story.

I don’t know how closely you follow the Middle Eastern news, but in 2002 an Israeli army went into the town of Bethlehem and surrounded the Church of the Nativity, where Jesus was born. That night I’d been asked to speak to the National Doctors Association of Lebanon, 800 doctors. It was on the day that a little boy was shot in his father’s arms. It went to the news around the world, a little boy at the Church of the Nativity was shot by an Israeli soldier, and the people of Lebanon were so angry at Israel and America. And then I was supposed to speak that night to 800 Lebanese doctors. It was my country that provided the guns to the army that shot the boy, and then I was supposed to speak that night. It was a little bit awkward. (laughter) I know that it’s not this bad here, but I heard that even today you had some troubles here in Basque Country with similar situations, and I’m sorry about that. It was that night, after we’d been there several years, when I really started to understand the difference between Jesus—the person Jesus the Nazareth, the Palestinian Jewish Jesus of Nazareth—between that person and how he treated people and between the religion that I came from—that had power and guns behind it. I started to see the difference profoundly, actually, that night. It started a difficult journey because when you’re the person in power, when you come from the country of power, like an American does, or some other regions around here, the person of power feels like Jesus is their person, that they own Jesus, that he’s their religious guy, and they use their religion, actually, to sometimes even oppress people. I started to realize all the connections, they all started to come crashing into my brain. Jesus was always on the side of the oppressed, the ones that were hurting, the ones in the worst situation. I don’t know what religion that is. I don’t know if there’s a religion “Jesus-ism”, I don’t know if there’s a religion that actually is Jesus, but whatever religion Jesus is all about that’s what we decided we wanted to follow. We actually want to follow him and his ways, rather than just the powerful religion I came from.

Jesus was a revolutionary. He was for the poor. He was for the ones who were being hurt, the hurting ones. He was for the powerless. And somehow I’d never thought of him that way. But it’s clear when you read the scriptures that that’s who the actual real Jesus is.

Now I’m guessing, not knowing you personally, that most of you are from a Catholic background, right?. Is that true? Yes, I guess that’s true. The issues are actually very similar from a protestant background, the protestant West, specifically America, has sometimes used religion in improper ways. And sometimes the Catholic West and Catholic East have used religion in improper or unhelpful ways. I think we can admit that. So, I think what we’ve kinda come to, is to say that our religion is our religion. Our religion is our culture, it’s part of us, and that’s fantastic. But there’s another thing called the Way of Jesus, which is another thing, and maybe is bigger than our religions. I know that’s a big thought, maybe it’s a controversial thought. I know the people in power in my country, the religious leaders in power, don’t like it when we talk like this because it feels like we’re taking the power away from the religious leaders and giving it back to Jesus. But I think that’s where it belongs.

So, that’s kinda the big thought that I had to share. I mean I could share more about what we actually do; with Israelis and Palestinians we do peace-making events. We use the teachings and life of Jesus, the person of Jesus, not the Christian version or the Muslim version or the Jewish version of Jesus, but the actual person of Jesus. We use the teachings of Jesus of Nazareth as a platform for peace-making between Israelis and Palestinians, that’s kinda what we do. We find that Jesus himself and his teachings are a great unifier of people whereas religions tend to divide people.

Jonathan, is there anything else that you wanted me to hit on or focus on before we open it up for questions?

–Yeah, can you tell us some interesting experiences, funny or profound, that you’ve had?

We’ve had lots of experiences. Mostly funny, not profound. (laughter) I’ll tell you one story that does summarize how crazy our life is. Have you heard of the Arab League? It’s like the United Nations, but in the Arab world? They asked me to come present a paper at their main Arab League meeting in Baghdad, Iraq about ten months ago. The conference was focused on injustice, which was really about the Palestinian situation, focusing on the injustices being done against the Palestinians. They wanted me to write a paper about injustice. I try to take everything and just add Jesus to it, so I wrote a paper called “Jesus’ Views on Injustice.” And after lots of discussion, they agreed that I could write a paper for the Arab League and present it in Baghdad on Jesus’ thoughts and views on injustice.

So what’s funny is that when I got there to Baghdad, they asked me if I’d be the chairman of the first meeting. I had no idea they’d ask me that question. I was basically in charge of the first meeting, which is really funny. So, I did. The prime minister of Iraq was there, the president was there, the head of the United Nations was there. And I was the chairman, and I didn’t even know what I was doing. (laughter) And I had written a very controversial paper. It was so controversial they didn’t put my name on it because people were so angry about the paper that they wanted to hurt the person who wrote it. So they didn’t put the person’s name on it, but it was my paper. And I was glad they didn’t put my name on it. (laughter)

But they liked the way I was running the meeting, so they asked me to chair another meeting, and then they asked me to chair another meeting, and then I ended up being the chairman of all the Arab League meetings. And that’s really funny. If you know me, that’s really funny, actually.

It’s fun when someone translates for you because I’m always wondering if you’re translating anything that I’m saying or if you’re just making up a story. Make it better! You’re telling jokes…

So after three days of being the chairman of these meetings, then the General Secretary of the Arab League asked me to come and to present my paper and to do it in my name. I said, “Are they going to kill me?” He said, “Probably not.” (laughter)

So here’s the thesis of the paper and this is why it was so controversial. The point of my paper was that the Palestinians or whoever is being acted against unjustly, who is receiving the injustice, who is being oppressed, they can choose to follow the Way of Jesus, they can choose to forgive. That’s as controversial, I think, for you to think about here as it was for them to think about there. Why the paper was controversial and what I was saying was controversial is because I was asking the Arab League to consider the Way of Jesus and the way of forgiveness. The way of Jesus talks about loving your enemies and forgiving those who persecute you. Now religion doesn’t necessarily do that, but the way of Jesus asks for that. And you, as Basques, can feel how controversial that thought is, can’t you?

Imagine, for three days we’ve been hearing of Palestinians who have had their children killed by Israeli soldiers. The title of my paper was “How the Palestinians Can Win.” That was the title of my paper. Which made all of them read the paper. (laughter) But the catch was that, in my opinion, the way of Jesus, not necessarily the way of Christianity—I’m trying to make that distinction—but the way of Jesus asks for those who have been hurt and oppressed to forgive, and when they do forgive they get set free.

And they were angry. And they killed me. (laughter) I just made that part up. What’s interesting is that after I talked about it for a while, and I asked them to consider it, just think about this way of Jesus. Don’t necessarily do it, just think about that Jesus asked the ones who had been hurt to forgive the ones who had hurt them. Just think about it. And think about how much power it actually gives you, the ones who are oppressed, it gives you power over your enemy when you choose to forgive them.

Because Jesus did not ask them to be run over or to be killed or to be oppressed; he wanted to give the oppressed people power, and the way to get power is to choose to forgive your enemies. Then you have power over your enemy. It’s actually a very politically clever idea by Jesus. But it’s very difficult, very controversial.

And so I said all this to the Arab League and all 500 of the delegates that were there. There were two ladies that were there from Gaza who were dressed in black because they were in mourning because their two boys had been killed the last month. When I finished my talk there was a minute of silence, nobody spoke for a long time. I wasn’t sure if they were going to rush the stage and beat me up or what they were going to do. (laughter) But after about a minute of silence, the place erupted in a standing ovation, they all jumped to their feet and started shouting and clapping and hollering and whistling. And these two ladies dressed in black whose sons—little boys 6 years old and 2 years old who had been killed by Israeli snipers just the month before—they were leading the charge running forward towards me. I actually took a step back, because I thought this was it. These two ladies ran up and they grabbed both of my hands and they put their hands on top of my hands and then they put all of our hands up to their faces and they just cried. They cried and cried. They kept saying in Arabic, “This is what we need. This is what we need. This is what we need.” The one lady kept saying, “The other thing isn’t working. The other thing isn’t working.” They were Muslims, they’re not Christians. They were Muslims, not Catholics, not Protestants. They were Muslims, these ladies. And I said, “What do we need? What is this thing that we need?” And they said, “This way of Jesus.”

It feels difficult for me to give this talk. It’s surprisingly difficult to give this talk here. I don’t if I want to keep talking; maybe I’ll stop talking now. It feels different. When I tell that story in America, everyone says, “Oh, that’s a nice story.” But saying this story to Basque people, it feels different. (laughter) So I understand, I do understand.

Really, for 30 years we’ve been on the side of the Palestinians. We’ve been American conservative Christians who usually….Right, you know this? Usually we are pro-Israel, my people are pro-Israel. And we’ve been the only people who are more pro-Palestinian, all these years. So I find what happens, is that you fall in love with the people, like the Palestinians, and I would take all their offense on me. I was offended on their behalf by Israel. I started to get more angry at Israel than they were, I was more against Israel than they were. I was more against America, my own country, my own government, I was more angry with my own country than they were, than the Palestinians were. So I, also, found it very difficult to ask the Palestinians to consider forgiving the Israelis. It didn’t sound right, even to me, let alone to them. So I feel how this sounds to you.

It’s funny, just growing up thinking that I might be Basque has made me always think about the Basque people. And I’ve thought for many years, “I’ll bet the Basque people are just like the Palestinians. People think that you’re the bad guys, but I know better. I know that’s not true.” I grew up thinking that, actually. Jesus is for you. That’s a nice thought, isn’t it? Just to think about that. That Jesus, the person of Jesus is for you, he’s for the Basque people. I like that thought. You should like that thought, too.

Carl Medearis, Diario Vasco article in Euskera
